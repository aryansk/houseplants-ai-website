Our Story

It started with a
dead pothos.

HousePlants wasn't planned, pitched, or funded. It's an indie app that grew out of one person killing plants, getting annoyed about it, and refusing to stop until the spreadsheet became an app. This is the honest timeline — failures included.

2021

The graveyard years

Like a lot of people stuck inside in 2021, I (Aryan — hi) filled my apartment with plants. A monstera, two pothos, a fiddle-leaf fig that cost more than my desk chair. Within six months, most of them were dead or dramatically dying.

I did what every plant parent does: googled "yellow leaves," got twelve contradictory answers, and overwatered out of guilt. The fig never forgave me.

2022

The spreadsheet of shame

Round two. This time I tracked everything in a spreadsheet: pot sizes, watering dates, which window each plant sat in, what the leaves looked like each week. It was ugly. It also worked — for the first time, things stopped dying.

The lesson stuck with me: plant care isn't hard, it's just specific. A snake plant in a 4-inch pot by a north window is a completely different problem from the same plant in a 10-inch pot in full sun. Generic advice kills plants. Calibrated advice saves them.

"What if the spreadsheet just… knew the answers?"
2023

Nights, weekends, and a very rough prototype

I started building an iOS app after work. The first version was embarrassing: a list of 40 plants and a watering reminder that fired at random times. I showed it to friends. They were polite, which is worse than honest.

But two ideas from that prototype survived everything since: the app should work completely offline, and it should never ask you to make an account. Your plants live in your home; your data should too.

Early 2024

Going deep on the database

I spent months doing the unglamorous part: building a real botanical reference. Cross-checking care ranges, toxicity data, light requirements, and humidity tolerances species by species. The database grew from 40 plants to 200, then past 500.

Somewhere in there I learned to measure light properly, and the AR Sun Seeker was born — point your camera at a spot, get an actual reading instead of guessing whether "bright indirect" applies.

Mid 2024

The doctor will see you now

The feature I wanted most was the one I'd needed in 2021: something to answer "why is this leaf yellow?" with an actual diagnosis instead of a forum argument. The Plant Doctor became a symptom-based wizard — discoloration, droop, pests — that yields ranked probable causes and fixes.

Beta testers used it more than everything else combined. That told me what the app really was: not a plant encyclopedia, but a set of calibrated instruments.

Late 2024

Launch (quietly)

HousePlants shipped to the App Store with no launch video, no press list, and no idea what would happen. The first week brought a handful of downloads, mostly friends. Every single review was read approximately forty times.

Then a plant subreddit found the toxicity checker, and for one strange weekend the download graph did something I had to screenshot to believe.

2025

Listening, fixing, repeating

The year of iteration. Users asked for an NPK fertilizer doser, so I built one. They asked for pet-safety data on every species, so the toxicity index went app-wide. They asked for moon gardening and I said no, then read the emails again, then built it anyway. (It's surprisingly popular. I don't ask questions.)

The illustrated species library passed 178 hand-finished plants, and the design found its identity — hard geometry, primary colors, no fluff. If Mondrian had kept houseplants, this is the app he'd have wanted.

2026

Still growing

Today HousePlants is still a one-person indie app, still offline-first, still account-free. This website now carries free web versions of the core tools — the Plant Doctor, Water Calculator, Fertilizer Doser, and Toxicity Checker — because the point was never downloads. It was fewer dead plants.

The fiddle-leaf fig? I bought another one. It's thriving. The story isn't finished — and neither is the app.
